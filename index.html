GenFit

AI health & fitness platform · brief to App Store in 8 weeks

Built and tailored for a large US gym chain; the client reports 1,300+ of their members onboarded so far

The brief was 500 words: be on both stores fast, while the backend was still being designed. I was in the client meetings from the very first call and shaped those conversations into an MVP, and owned everything through to release, privacy policy included.

  • Went through MyFitnessPal, Hevy and Strong screen by screen on Mobbin, plus their App Store reviews, so I wasn't repeating onboarding mistakes users were still complaining about. The client wanted both at once: a personal trainer's hand on the diet and workout, backed by an AI-generated plan. That's the shape GenFit took
  • Cut streaks, leaderboards, and Oura and MyFitnessPal integrations from v1 on purpose (good v2 ideas, not launch-blockers) and built it in React Native so one codebase could hit both stores on an 8-week clock
  • First pass at onboarding was 32 fixed questions, too many. Turned it into a branching flow that shows about 26 to any one person by skipping whatever their earlier answers already ruled out
Measured time to finish onboarding, after the branching redesign above: down about 20%
  • Scoped it into 51 delivered cards, made the architecture call that let the app get built before the backend existed, shipped health data on both Apple HealthKit and Android Health Connect, and set the quality bar at 978 unit and contract tests (Jest, runnable without a backend or device) before submitting to the store myself

BillPay-POS

Healthcare iPad point-of-sale · compliance-first delivery

One of Lab3's own products: a small commission on every payment means the company still tracks it, $50k+ a month across live clinics

Clinics were already running this as a web app at the front desk. The ask was to move it to iPad, and add a kiosk mode next to it so a patient could pay without a staff member touching their card. No design file, no handoff, just the existing web app to study and a solo iOS developer to work out PCI and HIPAA compliance well enough to pass clinic standards.

  • The kiosk was its own product problem: a patient paying alone needed a stricter version of every rule the front desk followed. Designed it so no receipt or card detail outlives the transaction it belongs to, and so no one, not even clinic staff, can see the payment behind a completed kiosk transaction
  • Scoped and shipped three payment paths (payment terminal, Bluetooth card reader, and manual cash/check/keyed entry) at the desk, plus a self-serve reader flow at the kiosk, none of which existed in the old web system
  • No internal resource existed for the AnyPay reader, so I got on calls directly with AnywhereCommerce's engineering team until the integration was clear enough to build against
  • Six audited actions on every touch of patient data, written into an append-only log that's never deleted, built into the product from day one rather than bolted on before a compliance review
  • Designed multi-tenant credential isolation with the backend team, so one clinic can never spend another's money (the session-expiry-vs-payment rule is under "How I decide" below)
  • Added offline payment caching and search the old web app never had, then handed it off properly: docs, credentials, a list of known issues and a live walkthrough, so the partner company could pick up the rollout without me

How I decide

calls that actually happened

Not principles I'd recite in an interview, just specific moments.

Cut on purpose

On GenFit, cutting scope came down to one filter: does this block launch, or does it just feel like it should be there? Streaks didn't pass, a good idea for a version 2 that has time to spare. The 8-week clock stayed real instead of aspirational.

Sequence by risk

When I had 160 tickets to plan across two products and the Harp platform, I didn't start with the easy ones. I started with whatever I was least sure I could pull off, so if the timeline was wrong, I'd find out in week one instead of week six.

Resolve conflicts with a rule

On BillPay-POS, a 60-minute session expiry could land mid-payment. Rather than patch it case by case, I set a standing rule: the session yields, the payment never does.

Client pushback

Simplified, not skipped

The client's plan was to ship GenFit skeletal now and add the real functionality behind features like HealthKit and camera access later, through updates. That doesn't hold up under Apple's review: a permission has to actually be demonstrated in use, not just declared, so bolting one on in a later update means a full review, not the fast lane. Once I walked the client through that, we shipped it differently: every feature that would eventually need a permission went out in a real, working, simplified form from day one, honest with Apple's reviewers, just not at the depth we actually wanted yet. Every update since has only had to grow those same features. No new permissions, no full review, just the fast lane.

How I work

what i bring to a product team

All of it learned on real products with real deadlines, mostly by getting something wrong first.

Requirements & stakeholders

On GenFit I was in the client calls from day one. On the others I usually got a short readme or an existing product to study and not much else. Either way it came down to the same thing: work out what people actually needed, turn that into scope, and explain the reasoning back in plain terms. Then kept sending written updates so nobody had to come ask for one.

Client discovery Stakeholder communication Recurring status updates US ↔ BD timezones

Scoping & delivery

160 tickets across two shipped products and the Harp platform lived in one Trello and Jira backlog, run in two-week sprints. An AI agent I built watched for a card marked done and auto-wrote it up three ways: dependencies for the next developer, acceptance criteria for QA, and a plain-language why for product, so Trello stayed accurate without manual write-up. The riskiest ticket in the sprint always got picked up first.

Backlog ownership Agile · Scrum · Trello · Jira PRDs & acceptance criteria Risk-first sequencing

Quality as a process

On Harp, I cared more about the bug that hadn't shown up yet than the one right in front of me. That meant manual QA on every page, growing the Playwright suite from 220 to 298 tests, GitHub Actions posting results to Slack after every PR, and scheduled runs that point straight at whatever broke. PRs got reviewed and merged in the order their testing cleared, never ahead of it.

Manual + automated QA CI/CD · GitHub Actions Slack-synced PR results Sentry monitoring

About

how i got here

I graduated in Computer Science from Khulna University of Engineering & Technology and went almost straight into a company where I was the only developer on the products I owned, and where instructions were scarce. GenFit showed up as a 500-word readme; BillPay-POS as someone else's web app to reverse-engineer. My job was to find the product inside them: figure out what to build from almost nothing, then turn that into something a team could actually ship. Nobody was going to do the product job if I didn't, so I learned it by doing it, with real money and real deadlines attached.

Three experiences taught me three different things. BillPay-POS is where I actually learned UX: designing a kiosk flow for a patient with no training and no one to ask forced me to think through a real journey, not just a screen. GenFit is where the business side caught up, client deadlines, requirements pulled out of a call instead of handed to me, and enough of a sense for what a paying customer needs to shape a product around it. Harp is where quality stopped being a task and became a discipline: a test suite that lies to you means the process itself has failed, and enough time on the QA side of things taught me what a developer's good instincts actually look like.

Before any of that I was General Secretary of the CSE Association at KUET, running events and budgets for 500+ students. The same organising habit shows up now in how I plan a release. I play table tennis badly and write about what I'm learning.
